What became of the abbeys?

Henry VIII closed the monasteries of England and Wales between 1536 and 1540. I traced {{n_houses|int}} of them to what stands on their sites today, then tested an old guidebook claim: that the Cistercians chose wild, empty places.

Every ruined abbey in Britain has the same opening scene. The commissioners arrive, the monks sign the surrender, the lead comes off the roof, and the stone is carted off to build somebody's house. But that is the story of the ruins, and ruins are only one of the things that happened. Some abbey churches became cathedrals. Many more became the parish church. Some were turned into country houses that still carry the word Abbey on the gate. Some became farms, schools, pubs and car parks. Many vanished.

I wanted to see the whole pattern rather than the famous few. So I built a list of every religious house closed between 1536 and 1540 that I could find in the Wikipedia county lists of monastic houses and in Wikidata: {{n_houses|int}} of them, {{n_england|int}} in England and {{n_wales|int}} in Wales. For each one I recorded the order, the year it closed, and what the lists say about the site now.

Then I used the same map to test something guidebooks say about the Cistercians, the white monks of Fountains, Rievaulx and Tintern: that they sought out remote, empty valleys, far from other people. Domesday Book gives a census of sorts for 1086, before almost all Cistercian houses were founded, so I can ask how many households lived near each future abbey site before the monks arrived.

    02 · Four and a half yearsThe houses fell in two waves

    The closures came in two waves. The Act of 1536 took the smaller houses, those worth less than £200 a year: {{by_year.1536|int}} houses in my list closed in 1536 and {{by_year.1537|int}} in 1537. Then came the great surrenders, house by house. {{by_year.1538|int}} houses closed in 1538, the year the friars went: {{friars_1538|int}} of the {{by_order.Friars|int}} friaries in my list closed that year. {{by_year.1539|int}} more closed in 1539, the Benedictines' worst year ({{benedictine_1539|int}} of their houses), and the last {{by_year.1540|int}} in 1540, among them {{military_1540|int}} houses of the Knights Hospitaller and other military orders.

    03 · AfterlivesHouses, churches, ruins, and nothing at all

    For {{afterlife_known_n|int}} houses the lists say clearly what is on the site now. Among those, the most common fate is a house: {{afterlife.house|int}} sites ({{afterlife_known_share.house|pct0}}) have a mansion, manor house or other home built on or out of the monastery, sometimes now a hotel. Close behind come churches still in use: {{afterlife.church|int}} sites ({{afterlife_known_share.church|pct0}}), where the nave, the choir, or a later church on the same ground still serves a parish. Another {{afterlife.cathedral|int}} became or remain cathedrals.

    Ruins, the image we all carry, account for {{afterlife.ruin|int}} sites ({{afterlife_known_share.ruin|pct0}}). Nothing visible is left at {{afterlife.nothing|int}} ({{afterlife_known_share.nothing|pct0}}), and only earthworks at {{afterlife.earthworks|int}}. In all, {{standing_share_of_known|pct0}} of the sites with a recorded fate still have something standing, and {{in_use_share_of_known|pct0}} are in some kind of use. Just {{afterlife.monastic|words}} are monasteries again.

    That leaves a large grey area, and I want to be plain about it. For {{afterlife.unclear|int}} houses the list rows say nothing about the present, and for {{afterlife.protected|int}} more the only clue is that Wikidata records a listed building or scheduled monument on the site, which tells us something survives but not what.     Each order went its own way

    The orders met very different ends. The Benedictines, the oldest order, often sat in towns with a parish attached, and their great churches were the easiest to keep: {{afterlife_known_share_by_order.Benedictine.church|pct0}} of Benedictine sites with a recorded fate have a church in use, and {{afterlife_known_share_by_order.Benedictine.cathedral|pct0}} are cathedrals. The Cistercians are the opposite. Their churches served no parish, and a third of their sites ({{afterlife_known_share_by_order.Cistercian.ruin|pct0}}) are ruins. The friars, in the middle of towns, were built over: {{afterlife_known_share_by_order.Friars.nothing|pct0}} of friary sites with a recorded fate have nothing visible left.

    04 · The Cistercian questionDid the white monks choose empty places?

    The Cistercian rule told monks to build far from towns and villages, and the order's own writers liked to describe their sites as wilderness. Guidebooks repeat it. Wikipedia's article on the order says the thirteen Cistercian houses in Wales were "all in remote locations". But a founder's grant was usually land someone already owned and farmed, and some Cistercian houses are said to have moved or cleared villages to make way for their granges. So how empty were these places before the monks came?

    Domesday Book (1086) records the households of thousands of places in England, {{domesday_located_places|int}} of which can be located today. It was made a generation or more before the first Cistercian house in England in 1128. For every house in the parts of England where the survey gives household counts, I added up the 1086 households recorded within 5 km. Because some counties were far busier than others in 1086, I then ranked each site against 400 random spots in its own county. A site at the 50th percentile was exactly as busy as a typical spot in that county; a site at the 10th was emptier than nine in ten.

    The answer is a qualified yes, and the qualification matters. The typical Cistercian monastery stood at the {{setting.Cistercian monks.pct_median|f0}}th percentile of its county: about as busy as an ordinary spot in the countryside. The Benedictine monks' houses stood at the {{setting.Benedictine monks.pct_median|f0}}th, the Augustinian canons' at the {{setting.Augustinian canons.pct_median|f0}}th and the friars, who wanted towns, at the {{setting.Friars.pct_median|f0}}th. In raw numbers, the typical Cistercian site had {{setting.Cistercian monks.hh5_median|f0}} households recorded within 5 km in 1086, against {{setting.Benedictine monks.hh5_median|f0}} for Benedictine monks.

    The Cistercians did not find the wilderness. They found ordinary countryside, while everyone else was in the villages and towns.

    The gap is real but modest. Comparing houses in the same county, Cistercian sites had about {{regression.effects.Cistercian monks.pct_change|abs}}% fewer 1086 households within 5 km than Benedictine sites (95% interval {{regression.effects.Cistercian monks.ci.1|abs}}% to {{regression.effects.Cistercian monks.ci.0|abs}}% fewer). Only {{cist_vs_ben.share_cist_bottom_quarter|pct0}} of Cistercian sites were in the emptiest quarter of their county, hardly more than the {{cist_vs_ben.share_ben_bottom_quarter|pct0}} of Benedictine sites. The order with genuinely empty sites was a different one: the Carthusians, the near-hermit monks of houses such as Mount Grace and Hinton, at the {{setting.Carthusian.pct_median|f0}}th percentile, though there are only {{setting.Carthusian.n|words}} of them in the comparison.

    Yorkshire, the Cistercian heartland, needs a warning. Much of it was recorded as waste in 1086 after William the Conqueror's harrying of the north, so every kind of house there sat in thin country. Within Yorkshire the {{yorkshire.Yorkshire.Cistercian monks.n|words}} Cistercian sites and {{yorkshire.Yorkshire.Benedictine monks.n|words}} Benedictine sites look alike (the {{yorkshire.Yorkshire.Cistercian monks.pct_median|f0}}th and {{yorkshire.Yorkshire.Benedictine monks.pct_median|f0}}th percentiles of their county). Outside Yorkshire the gap is clearer: the {{yorkshire.Rest of England.Cistercian monks.pct_median|f0}}th against the {{yorkshire.Rest of England.Benedictine monks.pct_median|f0}}th. If I widen the test to every house in the lists with a known order, whenever it closed, the picture holds: the {{robust_all_listed.Cistercian monks.pct_median|f0}}th percentile for {{robust_all_listed.Cistercian monks.n|int}} Cistercian sites against the {{robust_all_listed.Benedictine monks.pct_median|f0}}th for {{robust_all_listed.Benedictine monks.n|int}} Benedictine ones.

    Where the Cistercians do stand out is in the ground itself. Their sites sit higher (a median of {{setting.Cistercian monks.elev_median|f0}} m against {{setting.Benedictine monks.elev_median|f0}} m for Benedictine monks) in hillier country: the ground within 1 km rises and falls by {{setting.Cistercian monks.relief_median|f0}} m against {{setting.Benedictine monks.relief_median|f0}} m. In Wales, where Domesday is silent, the difference is starker: {{wales_terrain.Cistercian monks.relief_median|f0}} m of relief around the {{wales_terrain.Cistercian monks.n|words}} Cistercian houses against {{wales_terrain.Benedictine monks.relief_median|f0}} m around the {{wales_terrain.Benedictine monks.n|words}} Benedictine ones. The white monks' valleys were steep, and that may be what made them feel remote.

    05 · Close to homeWhalley and Sawley

    Two Cistercian houses stood {{local.distance_km|f0}} km apart in the Ribble valley. Sawley was founded in 1147 as a daughter house of Newminster in Northumberland, and Wikipedia calls it "a relatively poor establishment". Whalley's monks came from Stanlow on the Mersey estuary, moving to Whalley in 1296. Both were caught up in the Pilgrimage of Grace, the great northern rising of 1536.

    Sawley was closed in 1536 as a small house, and its monks were put back in by the rebels that October. Whalley's last abbot, Paslew, was executed for high treason for his part in events around the rising, and the abbey was taken in March 1537. Today their afterlives split along the usual lines. Sawley is a ruin, open to the public in English Heritage's care. At Whalley only the foundations of the church remain, a large house was built where the abbot's house and infirmary stood, a gateway is listed Grade I, and the whole is a retreat and conference house of the Church of England. My Domesday comparison cannot reach either: modern Lancashire has almost no household counts in the survey. Domesday Book does name {{local.Sawley Abbey.dom_n5|words}} places within 5 km of Sawley and {{local.Whalley Abbey.dom_n5|words}} within 5 km of Whalley, so neither valley was empty of settlement.

    06 · MethodHow I did this

    The houses. The core list is the set of English Wikipedia county lists of monastic houses: 46 English county pages and the Welsh page. The analysis machine could reach only GitHub, so I read each English page with a web reader instructed to copy, row by row, the name, the status mark after it, the communities in bold, the words after "dissolved", every later-use clause verbatim, and the coordinates. The Welsh and Lancashire pages were also available as raw wikitext, which I parsed directly. As a check, the reader's copy of the Lancashire page agreed with the raw wikitext on all {{extraction_check.matched_names|int}} rows for the status mark, the order and the year. The Isle of Wight page could not be fetched, so its houses come from Wikidata only.

    I read {{list_rows_read|int}} rows, dropped {{list_rows_xref|int}} cross-references to other counties and merged {{list_rows_duplicates_merged|int}} rows that describe the same house twice. I joined each row to the Wikidata items for UK monasteries ({{wikidata_ew_items|int}} in England and Wales) by name and distance, which adds listed-building and scheduled-monument designations, and added {{n_wikidata_only|words}} houses closed 1536 to 1541 that are in Wikidata but not in the lists. Where both give coordinates, the median gap is {{coord_check.median_m|int}} m and {{coord_check.share_within_500m|pct0}} agree within 500 m. The main set is every house whose closing year falls in 1536 to 1541: {{n_houses|int}} houses. Wikipedia's article on the Dissolution says there were "nearly 900" religious houses in England, so my {{n_england|int}} English houses are roughly {{share_of_900|pct0}} of that figure. Part of the gap is surely among the {{list_rows_no_year|int}} list rows that give no dissolution year at all, which I leave out.

    The fate of each site. I classified the list's later-use text with fixed rules, in this order of priority: cathedral; monastery again; church in use; house or hotel; school or college; farm; other use (museum, inn, shops, offices and so on); ruin (including sites in the care of English Heritage, Cadw or the National Trust); earthworks only; nothing visible. Clauses that describe the past ("until", "demolished", "possibly") do not count. The list's own marks are used too: + (church use), ^ (other use), $ (earthworks), # (no trace), * (monastic). Where the list is silent, Wikidata's "monastery ruins" and "destroyed monastery" types fill in, and a protected designation alone gives "protected, use unrecorded". Everything else is "unclear". In a random check of {{audit.n|int}} classified houses against their source text, I judged {{audit.consistent|int}} consistent; two earlier rounds of checking were used to improve the rules. Each house's deciding phrase is in the data file, so any call can be checked.

    The order. The last community the list names before closure (so a Savigniac house that became Cistercian in 1147 counts as Cistercian). Nunneries are kept separate where the order allows. Friars of every kind are grouped together, as are the Templars and Hospitallers.

    1086 households. From the Hull Domesday Project's data as joined to Open Domesday places in the realm-domesday repository and my Domesday Destiny project: households recorded for every located Domesday place within 5 km of the site. Random comparison points: 400 per county, drawn inside the county outline. The regression is ordinary least squares on log(1 + households) with a fixed effect for each county and robust standard errors. Height and relief come from AWS Terrain Tiles (about 100 m pixels here); relief is the range of ground height across the site and 16 points 500 m and 1 km away.

    Caveats

    The Wikipedia lists are a volunteer compilation, uneven from county to county, and their notes on the present state of a site are often silent or out of date. My rules read words, not stones: a "house built on site" may hold no medieval fabric at all. Domesday Book is not a census, it is thin in the north and silent for Wales, much of Lancashire, London and Winchester, and it describes 1086, decades before most of these houses existed and centuries before they closed. The comparison says where the monks settled relative to where people lived in 1086, not whether they later emptied the land around them.
